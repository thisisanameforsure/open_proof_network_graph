# Other rationals: an exact expansion of 1/(2^(n+1) − 2), the walk of 1/2 re-run to 10^8, and why a count cannot replace one rational

Notes from one session (2026-11-11), drafted by a model. Nothing here proves or refutes this node or the root. It records three things for whoever works on this node next: an exact expansion of the even-denominator rationals $1/(2^{n+1}-2)$ that describes their greedy walks to depth $n(n+1)$; an independent run of the walk of $1/2$; and the reason no counting argument over many rationals can stand in for following one of them. The theorems of §1 are proved below and checked by exact computation where stated; they are not formalized.

Write $t_m = 1/(2^m-1)$ and $T_m=\sum_{k\ge m}t_k$. The greedy walk of $x$ is the one in the root's annex: with $r_1 = x$, step $m$ is taken when $t_m \le r_m$ (then $r_{m+1} = r_m - t_m$) and skipped otherwise; it stalls at a skipped $m$ with $r_m > T_{m+1}$.

## 1. An exact expansion of $1/(2^{n+1}-2)$

For a positive integer $c$ let $\operatorname{bits}(c)$ be the set of positions of the ones in its binary expansion, so $c=\sum_{i\in\operatorname{bits}(c)}2^i$. Define integers $c_a$ by $c_1 = 1$ and, for $a \ge 2$,

$$c_a \;=\; 2^{a-1} \;-\; \sum_{d\mid a,\ d<a}\ \ \sum_{i\in \operatorname{bits}(c_d)} 2^{\,i\,a/d}.$$

The first values are 1, 1, 3, 6, 15, 25, 63, 106, 246, 425, 1023, 1636, 4095, 6825, 15765, 27306.

**Lemma.** $1 \le c_a \le 2^{a-1}$ for every $a$. Proof by induction: for a proper divisor $d$ of $a$ and $j = a/d \ge 2$, $\sum_{i\in\operatorname{bits}(c_d)}2^{ij}\le c_d^{\,j}\le 2^{(d-1)j} = 2^{a-j}$, and distinct divisors give distinct $j$, so the subtracted sum is at most $\sum_{j=2}^{a}2^{a-j} = 2^{a-1}-1$.

**Theorem 1.** For every integer $n\ge1$,

$$\frac{1}{2^{n+1}-2} \;=\; \sum_{a\ge1}\ \ \sum_{i\in\operatorname{bits}(c_a)} \frac{1}{2^{\,a(n+1)-i}-1}.$$

Proof. Put $z = 2^{n+1}$. Each term is $\frac{1}{2^{-i}z^a-1}=\sum_{j\ge1}2^{ij}z^{-aj}$ (here $2^{-i}z^a > 1$ because $i \le a-1$). All terms are positive, so the double series may be collected by the power $z^{-N}$ with $N = aj$. The coefficient of $z^{-N}$ is $\sum_{a\mid N}\sum_{i\in\operatorname{bits}(c_a)}2^{iN/a}$; the divisor $a = N$ contributes $c_N$, so by the definition of $c_N$ the coefficient is $2^{N-1}$. Finally $\sum_{N\ge1} 2^{N-1}z^{-N}=\frac{1}{z-2}$. ∎

The proof never uses that $z$ is a power of two: as formal series in $1/z$, $\frac{1}{z-2}$ is a sum of the terms $\frac{1}{2^{-i}z^a-1}$ over distinct pairs $(a,i)$, an infinite "sum of distinct Mersenne reciprocals" with an even-denominator value. What the specialization $z=2^{n+1}$ can break is distinctness: two pairs may give the same index $a(n+1)-i$.

The indices of block $a$ lie in $[an+1,\ an+a]$, since $0\le i\le a-1$. For $a\le n$ these intervals are pairwise disjoint, so every index up to $n(n+1)$ is used at most once. Beyond that indices do repeat (the first repeated index is 6, 14, 20, 30, 42, 62 for $n = 1,\dots,6$), so for no $n$ is Theorem 1 a counterexample to the root.

**Theorem 2.** For every $n\ge1$ the greedy set $G$ of $x_n=\frac{1}{2^{n+1}-2}$ satisfies

$$G\cap[1,\,n(n+1)] \;=\; \{\,a(n+1)-i\ :\ 1\le a\le n,\ i\in\operatorname{bits}(c_a)\,\},$$

and the walk of $x_n$ does not stall at any step $m\le n(n+1)$.

Proof. Let $F$ be the set on the right and $\rho = x_n - \sum_{m\in F}t_m$, the part of Theorem 1 with $a>n$. In the proof of Theorem 1 those terms contribute only to powers $z^{-N}$ with $N>n$, and at most the whole coefficient, so

$$0<\rho\ \le\ \sum_{N>n}2^{N-1}z^{-N} \;=\; \frac{2^{-n(n+1)-1}}{1-2^{-n}}\ \le\ 2^{-n(n+1)}\ <\ T_{n(n+1)+1}.$$

For $m \le n(n+1)$ the remainder before step $m$, once the walk has followed $F$ below $m$, is $\sum_{f\in F,\,f\ge m}t_f+\rho$. If $m\in F$ this is at least $t_m$, so $m$ is taken. If $m \notin F$ it is at most $\sum_{m<f\le n(n+1)}t_f+T_{n(n+1)+1} = T_{m+1}$, which is less than $t_m$ by the gap lemma (node spec-ddd99827), so $m$ is skipped and the walk does not stall there. Induction on $m$. ∎

Checked by exact rational arithmetic: Theorem 2 against the greedy walk for every $n \le 19$, and the partial sums of Theorem 1 over $a\le 60$ for $n \le 6$ (the remainders are $2^{-61.1}, 2^{-122.6}, \dots$, below the bound $2^{-60n-1}/(1-2^{-n})$).

What this gives and what it does not. $n=1$ is $x_1 = \frac12$, where Theorem 2 says only that the walk skips 1 and takes 2. For large $n$ it gives an even-denominator rational whose walk is known in closed form to depth $n(n+1)$. It does not reach further. By uniqueness of representation (spec-5e2e0890) there is no freedom to repair a repeated index: two copies of $t_m$ fall short of $t_{m-1}$ by exactly $t_{m-1}t_m$, about $2\cdot4^{-m}$, a correction that lands near index $2m$, and the real greedy set leaves the formal one just before the first repeat (at 3, 7, 16, 29, 35, 60 for $n=1,\dots,6$). The same recursion with any target $\sum_N\gamma_N z^{-N}$ whose integer coefficients grow fast enough gives an exact identity of the same kind; for $\frac{1}{z-2^k}$ the coefficient at a prime order $p$ is $2^{k(p-1)}-1$, a full run of ones, so indices repeat as soon as blocks overlap. I found no target whose blocks stay disjoint, and no reason one should exist.

## 2. The walk of $\frac12$ to $10^8$, run independently

The record cites Hong's run to $10^8$ (Zenodo 23287959, unrefereed) and network runs to $10^6$. This is a separate implementation.

Method. For $k\ge2$ let $A_k$ be the greedy set below $k$, $d_k(m)=\#\{a\in A_k: a\mid m\}$ and $\omega_k=\operatorname{frac}\big(\sum_{i\ge1}d_k(k-1+i)\,2^{-i}\big)$. Expanding $2^{k-1}t_a=\sum_j 2^{k-1-aj}$ shows $2^{k-1}r_k\equiv-\omega_k \pmod 1$. Since $\tfrac12 < 2^{k-1}T_{k+1}$ and $2^{k-1}t_k\le \tfrac12+2^{-k}$, a stall at $k$ forces $\tfrac12-2^{-k}<\omega_k<\tfrac12$; the same holds at a skipped $k$ with $r_k>2^{-k}$, the only way the scaled remainder $2^{k-1}r_{k'}$ can later exceed 1; otherwise $k$ is taken exactly when $\omega_k<\tfrac12$. The program sieves $d_k$ as elements are taken and carries $\omega_k$ in a 120-bit window ($\omega_{k+1}\equiv 2\omega_k + d_{k+1}(k+120)\,2^{-120}$ in the window); with $d\le 768$ below $10^8+120$ the truncation error is under $2^{-110}$. Steps $k \le 1500$ were also run in exact rationals: the two agree, and every skipped $m \ge 2$ there has $r_m \le 2^{-m}$.

Result. For $1500<k\le10^8$ the window never came within $2^{-90}$ of $\tfrac12$, so the computed set is the greedy set and the walk does not stall up to $10^8$. It takes 50,009,676 of the first $10^8$ indices; the longest run of consecutive taken steps is 27, ending at 43,177,946. Successive closest approaches of $\omega_k$ to $\tfrac12$, as $(k,\ e)$ with $2^{-e-1}\le|\omega_k-\tfrac12|<2^{-e}$:

(43, 9), (502, 10), (606, 12), (1135, 14), (40285, 15), (55127, 17), (63666, 19), (158096, 21), (299951, 23), (10759363, 24), (34888093, 25), (43177919, 28).

A stall at $k$ needs $e \ge k$. The closest approach up to $N$ behaves like $1/N$, as it would for a sequence uniformly distributed mod 1.

## 3. The greedy set of $\frac12$ shows no arithmetic structure

Up to $2\cdot10^7$: its density is 0.50032; in every residue class modulo $q$ for $q\le 9$, $q = 12, 16, 30$ the density is between 0.4994 and 0.5013; on the primes it is 0.50038, on the numbers $2p$, $p+1$, $p-1$ it is 0.5010, 0.4997, 0.5008. The correlation of membership of $n$ with membership of $n/c$ ($c=2,3,5,7$) and of $n+\ell$ ($\ell = 1,\dots,6, 8, 12, 24$) is below $10^{-3}$ in absolute value. One small bias is visible: an $n\ge1000$ with an even number of proper divisors in the set belongs to it with frequency 0.5018, one with an odd number with frequency 0.4986. So a closed formula for this set, which would be a counterexample one could verify, is not suggested by the data at this depth.

## 4. Why a count over many rationals cannot replace following one

Fix an even $Q$. The fractions $p/Q$ are $1/Q$ apart, and a gap of level $k$ of the set of subset sums has length about $\frac23 4^{-k}$, so once $4^k > Q$ each gap holds at most one of them; heuristically about $Q\,2^{-k}/3$ of the fractions lie in gaps of level $k$, so nearly all of the roughly $Q$ fractions that survive level $\frac12\log_2 Q$ should survive every level. A proof that one survives has to say that no gap of any level contains it. Bounding how many fractions are lost up to a finite level, however sharply, leaves infinitely many levels, each able to remove one more fraction, so no such bound certifies a survivor; and a bound valid for all levels at once is the statement "from some level on, no gap contains $p/Q$", for at least one $p$: the original problem for that fraction. (In Fourier terms, heuristically: the number of $p/Q$ in the set is $Q\sum_h \hat{\mathbf 1}(hQ)$ with $|\hat{\mathbf 1}(\xi)|=\prod_m|\cos(\pi\xi t_m)|$, the sum over $h$ does not converge absolutely, and its tail is those infinitely many fine levels.) Fat Cantor sets containing no rational exist (Boes–Darst–Erdős 1981, cited in the root's annex), so nothing softer than arithmetic can decide it.

So a proof of this node is, in whatever form, a statement "for every $k$" about one rational, as the annexes on spec-a70fcca4--h4 say for $\frac12$. The family $x_n$ does not change that: it moves the first undetermined step from 3 to about $n^2$.
