# A forcing theorem behind four of the special cases, why forcing cannot reach the root, the literature, and the walk of 1/2 to 10^10 (2026-11-12)

Notes from one session (2026-11-12), drafted by a model. Nothing here proves or refutes the root. It records, for whoever works on the case-split route or on the refuting routes: one theorem with proof (not formalized) that contains the periodic, squarefree and two-squares cases already on the graph and several sets that are not; a precise reason no argument of that kind can prove the root; the neighbouring series for which a rational value *is* provable, and why that does not transfer; what the literature says as of this date; and a run of the walk of $\tfrac12$ to $10^{10}$.

Write $t_n = 1/(2^n-1)$, $S_A=\sum_{a\in A}t_a$ and $d_A(m)=\#\{a\in A: a\mid m\}$, so $S_A=\sum_{m\ge1}d_A(m)2^{-m}$.

## 1. The window identity

For every $A$ and every $n\ge0$,

$$2^nS_A \;=\; \sum_{m\le n}d_A(m)2^{\,n-m}\;+\;c_A(n),\qquad c_A(n)=\sum_{j\ge1}d_A(n+j)\,2^{-j}.$$

The first sum is an integer. So if $S_A=p/Q$ then $c_A(n)\in\frac1Q\mathbb Z$ for every $n$. Every irrationality proof on this target is a way to find one $n$ with $0<\operatorname{frac}\,c_A(n)<1/Q$.

## 2. The forcing theorem

Let $P$ be an infinite set of primes and, for an integer $m$, let $s_P(m)$ be the number of $p\in P$ dividing $m$ exactly once.

**Theorem.** Let $P=\{\pi_1<\pi_2<\dots\}$ satisfy $\liminf_k k^2\,2^{-k}\log\pi_{3k^2}=0$ (true for all primes and for the primes in any residue class). Let $A$ be a nonempty set of positive integers for which there is a constant $C\ge0$ with

$$2^{\,s_P(m)-C}\ \big|\ d_A(m)\qquad\text{for every } m\ge1. \tag{H}$$

Then $S_A$ is irrational.

**When (H) holds.** Call $A$ *toggle-invariant* for $P$ if, for $p\in P$ and $n$ not divisible by $p$, $n\in A\iff pn\in A$. Then (H) holds with $C=0$: if $p$ divides $m$ exactly once, the map on divisors of $m$ that multiplies by $p$ the divisors prime to $p$ and divides the others by $p$ is an involution without fixed points that preserves membership in $A$; the $s_P(m)$ involutions commute, so the divisors of $m$ in $A$ fall into orbits of size $2^{s_P(m)}$. If each toggle either preserves $A$ or exchanges $A$ with a fixed second set, the toggles in even number preserve $A$, and (H) holds with $C=1$.

**Proof of the theorem.** Suppose $S_A=p/Q$. Fix $k\ge4$, put $J=k^2$, and choose distinct primes $p_{j,i}\in P$, all larger than $J$, for $1\le j\le k$ and $1\le i\le j+C$; they can be taken among $\pi_1,\dots,\pi_{3k^2}$. Put $q_j=\prod_i p_{j,i}$, $M=\prod_j q_j^2$, and let $0\le a<M$ satisfy $a+j\equiv q_j \pmod{q_j^2}$ for every $j\le k$. For $n=a+Mx$ each $p_{j,i}$ divides $n+j$ exactly once, so $s_P(n+j)\ge j+C$ and by (H) $2^j\mid d_A(n+j)$ for $j\le k$. Hence

$$c_A(n)=\text{integer}+R(n),\qquad R(n)=\sum_{j>k}d_A(n+j)2^{-j}>0 .$$

It is enough to find $x$ with $R(n)<1/Q$. Bound $d_A\le d$, the divisor function, and average over $1\le x\le M$.

(i) For $j>k$ let $g=\gcd(a+j,M)$. A prime of $M$ dividing $a+j$ divides $j-j'$ for some $j'\le k$, and all primes of $M$ exceed $J$. So $g=1$ for $k<j\le J$, and for $j>J$ at most $k\log j/\log J$ primes divide $g$, each to exponent at most 2, whence $d(g)\le 3^{k\log j/\log J}\le 2^{j/2}$ (this uses $j>k^2$, $k\ge4$).

(ii) $n+j=g\,(b+M'x)$ with $\gcd(b,M')=1$, and $d(n+j)\le d(g)\,d(b+M'x)$. Counting each divisor pair by its smaller member, $\sum_{x\le M}d(b+M'x)\le 2\sum_{e\le\sqrt Y}(M/e+1)$ with $Y=b+M'M$, so for $j\le M$ the mean of $d(b+M'x)$ is at most $L=6+2\log(M+1)$.

(iii) For $j>M$ use $d(n+j)\le n+j$; that part of $R$ is below $2^{-M/2}$.

Together the mean of $R$ over $x\le M$ is at most $L\,(2^{-k}+4\cdot2^{-k^2/2})+2^{-M/2}\le 2L\,2^{-k}$, and $\log M\le 4k^2\log\pi_{3k^2}$. By the hypothesis on $P$ this is below $1/Q$ for some $k$, so some $x$ has $0<R(n)<1/Q$: a contradiction with §1. $\blacksquare$

The same proof gives the theorem for bounded non-negative integer weights $w$ in place of the indicator of $A$, with $(w*1)(m)$ in place of $d_A(m)$.

**What it contains.** This is Erdős's 1948 argument with the symmetry it uses made explicit. With $P$ all primes: all $n$; the squarefree numbers (variant-970a2ffa); the $k$-free numbers; $\{\mu=+1\}$, $\{\mu=-1\}$, $\{\omega \text{ even}\}$ (with $C=1$). With $P$ the primes $\equiv1 \pmod q$: every set periodic mod $q$, and so, after a finite change, every eventually periodic set (variant-5274cf38, variant-886b66da). With $P$ the primes $\equiv1\pmod4$: the sums of two squares (variant-d08712d9). Also the multiples of $K$, the integers prime to $K$, and any set defined through $n/\prod\{p\in P: p \text{ divides } n \text{ exactly once}\}$.

Checked numerically: (H) for seven of these sets for all $m\le30000$ (no violation); the construction at $k=3$ (six primes, $M\approx2^{50}$) for the squarefree numbers.

A by-product: $\sum_n\mu(n)t_n=\sum_m2^{-m}\sum_{d\mid m}\mu(d)=\tfrac12$, so $S_{\{\mu=+1\}}-S_{\{\mu=-1\}}=\tfrac12$ exactly. These are two explicit points of the set of subset sums, both irrational by the theorem, at distance exactly $\tfrac12$.

## 3. Why forcing cannot prove the root

The proof of §2 uses only $d_A\le d$ and the divisibility (H). It does not see whether the weights are in $\{0,1\}$ or in $\{0,1,2\}$. But with weights in $\{0,1,2\}$ the root's statement is false: the sequence $t_1,t_1,t_2,t_2,\dots$ has every term at most the sum of the later ones, so by Kakeya's theorem every real in $(0,2T_1]$ is $\sum_n w(n)t_n$ with $w(n)\in\{0,1,2\}$ and infinitely many $w(n)\neq0$; in particular $\tfrac12$ is. So an argument that proves irrationality for every infinite $A$ must use that no weight is 2, that is, the gap inequality $t_m>T_{m+1}$, whose margin is $\tfrac23 4^{-m}$. That is the same margin the greedy walk of the refuting routes lives on.

The other known methods need, of $A$: pairwise coprime elements and $\sum 1/a<\infty$ (Erdős 1968: exact values of $d_A$ on a window by the Chinese remainder theorem and a sieve); finitely many distinct shifts $A-j$ (Padé and $q$-difference methods: eventual periodicity); closure under $n\mapsto dn$ up to a finite set (Mahler's method). The greedy set of $\tfrac12$ has density $\tfrac12$ in every residue class tested and no visible symmetry (annex on variant-cce9b284), so none applies to it; and a toggle symmetry for it would contradict its sum being $\tfrac12$.

## 4. Neighbouring series where a rational value is provable

- $u_n=1/(2^n+1)$ satisfies $u_n\le\sum_{k>n}u_k$ for every $n\ge1$, so every real in $(0,\,0.7645\ldots]$, in particular every rational there, is the sum of $u_n$ over an infinite set (take $n$ exactly when $u_n$ is smaller than the remainder). Since $u_n=t_n-2t_{2n}$, there is an infinite $B$ with $\sum_{n\in B}(t_n-2t_{2n})=\tfrac12$.
- Signs: $\sum_n\mu(n)\,t_{kn}=2^{-k}$.
- Weights $\{0,1,2\}$: §3.

In each case the set of sums is an interval, and representations are not unique. With $2^n-1$ and weights $\{0,1\}$ the gap lemma (spec-ddd99827) removes exactly that freedom. Kovač and Tao (Acta Math. Hungar. 175 (2025), §2.1.2, Theorem 2.3) prove a rational value for several bases $t_1<\dots<t_m$ together when $\sum_k 1/(t_k-1)>1$; one base $t=2$ gives exactly 1, the critical case.

## 5. The literature as of 2026-11-12

- erdosproblems.com/257 (last edited 2026-04-15) lists the problem as open with no proof claim. No resolution in either direction was found.
- Erdős 1968 (quoted by Kovač–Tao): "I know no example of an infinite sequence $n_1<n_2<\cdots$ and $t\ge2$ for which $\sum 1/(t^{n_i}-1)$ is rational, though it seems likely that this can happen." He expected irrationality when $n_{k+1}-n_k\to\infty$. The form "perhaps irrational for every sequence" is Erdős–Graham 1980.
- Kovač–Tao, same section: "We do not know how to solve the original question implied by Erdős." Kovač (forum, 2025): the answer is "very likely negative", but measure alone cannot prove it.
- Two unreviewed notes prepared with AI assistance, read but not checked here: Hong (Zenodo 23287959) reduces non-stalling at $\tfrac12$ to the absence of a run of $(1-\delta)k$ equal binary digits at position $k$ of an explicit number; Cook (forum, 2026-09-11) states a criterion by "repairs" in every window $[K,K+2\lfloor\sqrt K\rfloor+12)$.
