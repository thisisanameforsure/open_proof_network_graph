## Statement and the helper lemmas

Write $t_m=\frac{1}{2^m-1}$ (with $t_0=\frac10=0$ in Lean). The statement is about a rational $x\ge 0$ and sequences $r:\mathbb N\to\mathbb R$, $q:\mathbb N\to\mathbb N$ with $r_0=x$, $q_m=\lfloor r_m/t_m\rfloor$ and $r_{m+1}=r_m-q_mt_m$. Let

$$G=\Bigl\{m:\ q_m\ne 0\ \text{and}\ r_{m+1}\le\sum_{j\ge 0}t_{m+1+j}\Bigr\},\qquad e_m=q_m-\mathbf 1_G(m).$$

Two more hypotheses are given: the accounting identity $\sum_{n\in G}t_n=x-\sum_m e_mt_m$ (`hacc`), and that $e_m\ne 0$ for only finitely many $m$ (`hfin`). The claim is: either the denominator of $x$ is odd, or $G$ is infinite and $\sum_m e_mt_m$ is rational.

The proof uses only `hacc` and `hfin`; the hypotheses $x\ge 0$, $r_0=x$ and the recurrence are introduced and not used. It rests on four private lemmas.

`odd_den_div`: for an integer $z$, the rational $z/(2^m-1)$ has odd denominator. For $m=0$ it is $z/0=0$. For $m\ge 1$ the denominator divides $2^m-1$, which is odd.

`odd_den_sum`: a finite sum of rationals with odd denominators has odd denominator, by induction on the index set, since the denominator of $u+v$ divides the product of the denominators of $u$ and $v$.

`repair_sum_rat`: if $e:\mathbb N\to\mathbb R$ takes integer values and vanishes outside a finite set $S$, then $\sum_m e_mt_m$ equals a rational with odd denominator, namely $\sum_{m\in S}e_m/(2^m-1)$.

`chosen_sum_rat`: if $A$ is a finite set, then $\sum_{n\in A}t_n$ equals a rational with odd denominator.

## The repair sum is a rational with odd denominator {steps: hz hS s3}

Each $e_m$ is an integer: it is $q_m-1$ if $m\in G$ and $q_m$ otherwise (`hz`). By `hfin` the set $\{m:e_m\ne 0\}$ is finite, and $e_m=0$ outside it (`hS`). So `repair_sum_rat` gives a rational $s_2$ with odd denominator and

$$\sum_m e_mt_m=s_2 .$$

Now split on whether $G$ is infinite.

## If $G$ is infinite {steps: s4}

Then the second alternative holds, with $s=s_2$.

## If $G$ is finite {steps: s5}

By `chosen_sum_rat` there is a rational $s_1$ with odd denominator and $\sum_{n\in G}t_n=s_1$. Substituting both values into `hacc` gives $s_1=x-s_2$ as real numbers, hence $x=s_1+s_2$ in $\mathbb Q$ (`hx'`). The denominator of $s_1+s_2$ divides the product of the two odd denominators, so the denominator of $x$ is odd, which is the first alternative.
