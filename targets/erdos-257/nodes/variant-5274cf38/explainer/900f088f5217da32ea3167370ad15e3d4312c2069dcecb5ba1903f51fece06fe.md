## What the assembly shows

The sum $\sum_{n\in A}\frac{1}{2^n-1}$ is irrational for every infinite, eventually periodic $A$, provided it is irrational for every nonempty purely periodic set: an eventually periodic set differs from a purely periodic one in finitely many places, and the difference of the two sums is a rational number. Here $A$ is *eventually periodic* when there are $p\ge1$ and $N$ with $n\in A\iff n+p\in A$ for all $n\ge N$.

The partial has one hole, the purely periodic case. That hole was proved on the graph on 12 November 2026. The node itself also has a separate merged proof that does not go through this partial.

## The purely periodic case {steps: hper}

The hole: if $B$ is nonempty and there is $p\ge1$ with $n\in B\iff n+p\in B$ for every $n$, then $\sum_{n\in B}\frac{1}{2^n-1}$ is irrational. The partial's comment identifies it with Theorem 1.1 of Luca and Tachiya (2014) for the indicator of $B$ at $q=2$.

## The series converges {steps: hfs}

Put $f(n)=\frac{1}{2^n-1}$, with $f(0)=\frac10=0$ in Lean. Then $0\le f(n)\le 2\cdot(\tfrac12)^n$: for $n\ge1$ this is $2^n\le 2(2^n-1)$, which holds since $2^n\ge2$. So $\sum_n f(n)$ converges.

## Periodicity iterated {steps: hiter}

By induction on $k$: for $n\ge N$, $n\in A\iff n+pk\in A$.

## The purely periodic set $B$ {steps: hpN hBper hAB hBne hB}

Let $B=\{n : n+pN\in A\}$. Since $pN\ge N$, the periodicity of $A$ at $n+pN$ gives $n\in B\iff n+p\in B$ for every $n$. For $n\ge N$ the last step with $k=N$ gives $n\in A\iff n\in B$. An infinite $A$ has an element above $N$, which is then in $B$, so $B$ is nonempty. By the hole, $\sum_{n\in B}f(n)$ is irrational.

## The difference is a finite rational sum {steps: h1 h2 hsub hfin hrat}

Both $\sum_n \mathbf 1_A(n)f(n)$ and $\sum_n\mathbf 1_B(n)f(n)$ converge, so the series of differences converges to the difference of the sums. Its terms vanish for $n\ge N$, where $A$ and $B$ agree, so it equals $\sum_{n<N}\bigl(\mathbf 1_A(n)-\mathbf 1_B(n)\bigr)f(n)$, a finite sum of rational numbers.

## Conclusion {steps: key s15}

Hence $\sum_{n\in A}f(n)=\sum_{n\in B}f(n)+q$ with $q$ rational. If the left side were a rational $r$, then $\sum_{n\in B}f(n)=r-q$ would be rational, contradicting the hole.
