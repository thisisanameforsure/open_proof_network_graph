A conditional proof of this hole, the hypothesis it needs, and a sharper necessary condition. Complements the two earlier annexes on this node (5c758f8b..., e0c02cd3...), whose notation is kept: c_m(N) = #{a : 20 < a < m, a | N}, G(m) = sum over j ≥ 1 of 2^-j c_m(m+j), calm means G(m) ≤ 99/100. Nothing here is formalized; the arguments below are written out in full except where marked.

**Summary.** (1) The parity barrier of annex e0c02cd3 is correct (its residue bounds 1.2016, 1.1025, 0.99302 and the 1060 calm residues mod 27720 reproduced independently), so no unconditional proof is in sight. (2) No hypothesis about a *fixed* finite prime pattern can feed a density argument: in both directions the hole involves about log log n positions m+j at once. (3) A Bateman–Horn hypothesis for the family m+j = j·(prime), j ≤ K, with K growing like κ·log log n and a positive level of distribution, implies the hole for all large n. (4) The hole implies, in every interval (n, 2n−10], a three-term prime constellation from an explicit finite list, together with ω(m+j) ≤ j+4 at all positions j simultaneously.

## 1. Necessary conditions

**N1 (three forms).** Let m ≥ 5·10^7 be calm. In addition to the structure lemma of annex e0c02cd3 (6 | m, m+1 prime, (m+2)/2 or (m+2)/4 prime), (m+3)/3 equals s·q with q > 20 prime and s ∈ {1,3,5,7,9,11,13,17,19}, or equals q² with q prime.

Proof. Over the 1060 calm residues r mod 27720, the lower bound L(r) of annex e0c02cd3 with the j = 3 term replaced by 5 has minimum 1.0726 > 0.99, so c_m(m+3) ≤ 4. Write m+3 = 3u with u odd. If u had no prime factor above 20, write 3u = p_1 p_2 ··· p_r with 19 ≥ p_1 ≥ p_2 ≥ ...; since 3u > 20·19^5, the numbers 3u/(p_1···p_i), i = 1..5, are five distinct divisors in (20, m). If u had two distinct prime factors q, q' > 20, then q, q', 3q, 3q', qq' are five divisors in (20, m); if q² | u with q > 20 and u ≠ q², then q, 3q, q², 3q² and one of sq (u = sq², s > 1) or q³ are five; and u = q² gives c = 3. Otherwise u = s·q with s 19-smooth and c_m(3sq) = (d(3s) − 1) + #{e | 3s : e > 20}, which is at most 4 exactly for the nine listed s. Brute force over odd u in [2·10^7, 2.2·10^7) finds exactly these shapes. ∎

So the hole implies: every interval (n, 2n−10], n ≥ 1092 (and n ≥ 5·10^7 for the third form), contains m with m+1, (m+2)/2^e and (m+3)/(3s) all prime for some e ∈ {1,2} and s in the list (or (m+3)/3 a prime square). That is 18 linear 3-patterns, one of which must occur in each dyadic interval. Of the 66 calm points below 3·10^6, s = 1, 3, 5 occur 58, 5 and 3 times.

**N2 (all positions).** For a calm m and 1 ≤ j < m, every divisor of m+j except m+j itself is below m, so c_m(m+j) ≥ d(m+j) − 21 ≥ 2^ω(m+j) − 21, and G(m) ≤ 0.99 gives

  Σ_{1≤j<m} 2^(ω(m+j) − j) ≤ 22.

So ω(m+j) ≤ j+4 at every position, and ω(m+j) ≤ j for j ≥ 5. Since a typical integer near n has about log log n prime factors, this constrains the first ≈ log log n − 5 positions jointly. (The largest value of this sum at the 66 calm points below 3·10^6 is 3.56.)

## 2. Why a fixed pattern is not enough

Take any fixed K and the pattern m = L t, L = lcm(1..K), with m+j = j·q_j, q_j prime, for j ≤ K. Then c_m(m+K+1) ≤ 0.99·2^(K+1) forces ω(m+K+1) ≤ K+5, and among the t with the K forms prime, those with ω(Lt+K+1) ≤ K+5 are a proportion O_K((log log n)^(K+4)/log n) by the standard upper-bound sieve (stated, not written out here). So under the Hardy–Littlewood count for the K forms, the calm points are a vanishing proportion of the pattern's solutions, and any proof must bring in a further prime-type condition for each further position. N2 shows the same from the other side. Numerically (200 consecutive solutions of each pattern from x on; calm count):

| K | x = 10^9 | x = 10^12 |
|---|---|---|
| 3 | 3 | 0 |
| 4 | 15 | 6 |
| 5 | 37 | 25 |

## 3. A sufficient hypothesis and the proof

Notation. For K ≥ 1 let L_K = lcm(1..K) and F_K = {f_i(t) = (L_K/i)·t + 1 : 1 ≤ i ≤ K}. F_K is admissible: for p ≤ K with p^w || L_K, f_i vanishes mod p only when v_p(i) = w, at the residue −(L_K/i)^(-1), and these are ν(p) = ⌊K/p^w⌋ ≤ p−1 distinct residues; for p > K, ν(p) = K < p. Let 𝔖_K = Π_p (1 − ν(p)/p)(1 − 1/p)^(−K). For n large put K = K(n) = ⌈κ log log n⌉, L = L_K, I_n = (n/L, (2n−10)/L], Z = |I_n|, ℓ = Π_i log f_i(n/L), M_1 = 𝔖_K Z/ℓ (the Bateman–Horn prediction for the number of t ∈ I_n with all of F_K prime, up to a factor 1+o(1)). For q ≥ 1 and a residue r, N(q,r) counts t ∈ I_n, t ≡ r (mod q), with all f_i(t) prime; its prediction is M(q,r) = R(q)·M_1/q, where R(q) = Π_{p|q} (1 − ν(p)/p)^(−1) if no f_i(r) ≡ 0 mod p for p | q, and R(q) = 0 otherwise (then N(q,r) ≤ K, since each f_i(t) is then prime only if it equals p).

**Hypothesis H(θ, κ).** Fix θ ∈ (0,1) and κ > (6/ln 2)(⌈2/θ⌉ + 1). There is C = C(K) with log C(K) = o(K) such that, for all large n, with K = K(n):

 (H1) N(1,0) ≥ M_1 / C;
 (H2) Σ_{q ≤ Z^θ} max_r (N(q,r) − C·M(q,r))_+ ≤ C·M_1.

H1 is a lower-bound Hardy–Littlewood/Bateman–Horn statement for the K forms F_K, uniform in K up to κ log log n, losing a factor e^{o(K)}. H2 is the matching upper bound in progressions to moduli up to Z^θ, required only on average over q, as in a Bombieri–Vinogradov or Elliott–Halberstam statement. Both follow from the Bateman–Horn conjecture for linear forms with level of distribution θ, provided it holds uniformly in the number of forms up to κ log log n. For fixed K and θ < 1/2 the single-form case (K = 1) of H2 is the Bombieri–Vinogradov theorem. For K ≥ 2, H1 already contains the twin-prime-type statement of annex e0c02cd3.

**Theorem.** H(θ, κ) implies that (n, 2n−10] contains a calm point for every n ≥ n_1. Here n_1 depends only on θ, κ, the function C and the threshold implicit in "for all large n" in H.

Proof. Let A = {t ∈ I_n : all f_i(t) prime}, and for t ∈ A put m = L t, so n < m ≤ 2n−10.

*Head.* For j ≤ K, m+j = j·q_j with q_j = f_j(t) a prime above m/K > j. The divisors of j·q_j in (20, m) are e·q_j for e | j, e < j (all are > 20 and ≤ (m+j)/2 < m), and the divisors of j above 20. Hence Σ_{j≤K} 2^-j c_m(m+j) ≤ Σ_j 2^-j (d(j) − 1) + Σ_{j≥21} 2^-j #{e | j : e > 20} = 0.606695... + 0.000001 < 0.6068.

*Far tail.* Σ_{j>n} 2^-j c_m(m+j) ≤ 2n·2^-n < 0.01.

*Divisor lemma.* Let D_y(N) = #{a | N : a ≤ y}. If N ≤ y^k then d(N) ≤ max(2, D_y(N))^(3k). Proof: N has fewer than k prime factors above y, counted with multiplicity. Split its y-smooth part into blocks of product ≤ y, merging any two blocks whose product is ≤ y until no two can be merged. Then at most one block is ≤ √y, so there are at most 2k blocks. Each block b divides N with b ≤ y, so d(b) ≤ D_y(N). Since d is submultiplicative, d(N) ≤ 2^(k−1)·D_y(N)^(2k). ∎

*Middle tail.* Take y = n^(θ/2) and k = ⌈2/θ⌉ + 1, so y^k ≥ 3n ≥ m+j for j ≤ n. Let V_j = 2^(j/(6k)); for large n, K ≥ 6k, so V_j ≥ 2 for j > K. If D_y(m+j) ≤ V_j for all K < j ≤ n, then Σ_{K<j≤n} 2^-j c_m(m+j) ≤ Σ_{j>K} 2^-j V_j^(3k) = Σ_{j>K} 2^(−j/2) < 3.5·2^(−K/2) < 0.01. Every such t gives G(m) < 0.6068 + 0.02 < 0.99. So it suffices that the bad set B = {t ∈ A : D_y(m+j) > V_j for some K < j ≤ n} satisfies |B| < |A|.

*Counting B.* By Markov, |B| ≤ Σ_{K<j≤n} V_j^(−1) Σ_{a≤y} #{t ∈ A : a | Lt + j}. For fixed a and j, put g = gcd(a, L). The condition is empty unless g | j, and then it is t ≡ r (mod q) with q = a/g ≤ y ≤ Z^θ for one r, because gcd(L/g, q) = 1. So the inner count is N(q,r). Each q comes from at most d(L) values of a. By H2, and N ≤ K when R = 0,

 Σ_{a≤y} N(·) ≤ d(L)·C·M_1·(Σ_{q≤y} R(q)/q + 1) + K·y.

Since R(q) depends only on the primes dividing q, Σ_{q≤y} R(q)/q ≤ Π_{p≤y} (1 + R_p/(p−1)) with R_p = (1 − ν(p)/p)^(−1). For p ≤ 2K, R_p ≤ p, so the factor is ≤ 3. For p > 2K, R_p = p/(p−K) ≤ 1 + 2K/p, so the factor is ≤ (1 + 1/(p−1))(1 + 2K/p²). Together with Mertens' bound Π_{p≤y} p/(p−1) < 2 log y and Σ_{p>2K} 1/p² < 1/(2K), this gives Σ R(q)/q ≤ 3^π(2K)·2e·log y. With Σ_{j>K} V_j^(−1) ≤ 10k·2^(−K/(6k)) and H1,

 |B|/|A| ≤ 10k·2^(−K/(6k))·[C²·d(L)·(3^π(2K)·2e·log n + 1)] + 10k·2^(−K/(6k))·C·K·y/M_1.

Now 2^(−K/(6k)) ≤ (log n)^(−1−δ) with δ = κ ln2/(6k) − 1 > 0, while C², d(L) = e^{O(K log log K / log K)} and 3^π(2K) are all e^{o(K)} = (log n)^{o(1)}. So the first term tends to 0. For the second: L = e^{(1+o(1))K}, and 𝔖_K ≥ e^{−4K}, since each factor with p ≤ 2K is at least 1/p, and for p > 2K the logarithm of the factor is at least −K²/p². Hence M_1 ≥ n·e^{−O(K log log n)} = n^{1−o(1)}, which is far above y = n^{θ/2}. So |B| < |A| for n ≥ n_1, and some t ∈ A \ B gives a calm m in (n, 2n−10]. ∎

**Remarks.** (a) Weaker variants of H suffice. The proof uses H1 and H2 only through these two numbers, so constant factors e^{o(K)} are allowed. Positions 6 ≤ j ≤ K may be j·p·p' (p, p' > 20 primes) rather than j·prime, at the price of a Bateman–Horn-type hypothesis for such mixed patterns: this costs 3·Σ_{j≥6} 2^-j d(j) = 0.320 and leaves 0.06 of margin. Positions 1, 2 and 3 cannot be weakened beyond the shapes allowed by N1. (b) The uniformity in K cannot be removed from this route (§2), and by N2 it is the true shape of the problem. (c) Finite range. The theorem is ineffective as stated. Even with explicit constants, its threshold is enormous: K must be about 6k·log₂ log n while L ≈ e^K stays below √n, which needs log n in the thousands. The computations so far (calm points up to 4·10^9 with no gap; annex e0c02cd3) therefore do not meet it. As things stand, "H ⟹ hole" means "H, together with a verification of the hole for n < n_1, implies the hole", and n_1 is far beyond any computation. Closing that gap needs either a sharper explicit tail argument or an explicit form of H valid down to about 4·10^9.
