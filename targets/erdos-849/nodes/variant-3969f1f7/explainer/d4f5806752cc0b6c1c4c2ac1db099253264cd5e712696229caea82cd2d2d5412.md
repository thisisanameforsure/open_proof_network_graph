## Three statements about where a row's $k$ can lie {steps: hall}

Write $f(n) = e^{(\log n)^{5/6}}$ (natural logarithm). The proof rests on three statements, each one of the holes of this partial proof and a node of its own:

- **bounded $k$** (`--h1`): for all $K$ and $a$, at most $K$ rows $n$ hold $a$ as $\binom{n}{k}$ with $2 \le k \le K$ and $2k \le n$;
- **middle $k$** (`--h2`, open): there are $K$ and $B$ such that for every $a$ at most $B$ rows $n$ hold $a$ as $\binom{n}{k}$ with $K < k < f(n)$ and $2k \le n$;
- **interior** (`--h3`): there is $a_0$ such that for every $a \ge a_0$ at most $2$ rows $n$ hold $a$ as $\binom{n}{k}$ with $1 \le k$, $f(n) \le k$ and $2k \le n$.

Each is stated in its own branch, so none is a hypothesis of another. The proof takes the three apart into $K$, $B_1$ from the second and $a_0$ from the third, and shows the bound $B = 1 + K + B_1 + (a_0 + 2)$ on the number of rows holding any $a$.

## Every row holding $a$ satisfies $n \le a$ {steps: s2 key}

If $\binom{n}{k} = a$ with $1 \le k$ and $2k \le n$, then $n = \binom{n}{1} \le \binom{n}{k} = a$. The middle inequality is proved by induction on $j$ for $1 \le j \le \lfloor n/2 \rfloor$: $\binom{n}{1} \le \binom{n}{j}$, each step using that $\binom{n}{j} \le \binom{n}{j+1}$ while $j < n/2$ (Mathlib's `Nat.choose_le_succ_of_lt_half_left`).

## Splitting the rows by $k$ {steps: hfin hsub}

Fix $a$, and let $F$, $M$, $I$ be the three sets of rows above (bounded, middle, interior $k$). Every row $n$ holding $a$ at some $1 \le k \le n/2$ lies in $\{a\} \cup F \cup M \cup I$, by cases on its $k$: $k = 1$ gives $\binom{n}{1} = n = a$; otherwise $2 \le k \le K$ puts $n$ in $F$; otherwise $k > K$, and $k < f(n)$ puts $n$ in $M$ while $k \ge f(n)$ puts it in $I$. All four sets lie in $[0, a]$ by the previous section, so their union is finite.

## Counting {steps: s6 s7 s8 s9 s10}

The number of rows holding $a$ is therefore at most $|\{a\} \cup F \cup M \cup I| \le 1 + |F| + |M| + |I|$, the size of a union being at most the sum of the sizes. The first statement gives $|F| \le K$ and the second $|M| \le B_1$. For $|I|$, two cases: if $a \ge a_0$ the third statement gives $|I| \le 2$; if $a < a_0$, then $I \subseteq [0, a]$, which has $a + 1 \le a_0$ elements. Either way $|I| \le a_0 + 2$, and the total is at most $1 + K + B_1 + (a_0 + 2)$, a bound that does not depend on $a$.
