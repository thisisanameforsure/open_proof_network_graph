## Replace a dense set by its initial part {steps: h_first}

Write $n=6k+r$ with $0\le r\le5$. Since $n\ge18$, we have $k\ge3$. The length bound $\lfloor n/3\rfloor+1$ is $2k+1$ when $r\le2$ and $2k+2$ when $r\ge3$. In either case, the largest allowed odd length is $2k+1$.

Let $B=A\cap[1,6k+1]$. If $|B|>4k$, the first open claim applies to $B$ at the same requested length. It asks for distinct entries in cyclic order, with coprime neighbors including the last and first entries. Such an ordering in $B$ is also an ordering in $A$ because $B\subseteq A$.

## Identify the only case where the initial part loses too much {steps: h_last}

The density threshold for $n=6k+r$ is
$$
4k+\lfloor r/2\rfloor+\lfloor r/3\rfloor.
$$
For $r=0,1,2,3,4,5$, the term added to $4k$ is respectively $0,0,1,2,3,3$. The number of vertices available outside $[1,6k+1]$ is respectively $0,0,1,2,3,4$. For $r\le4$, deleting those vertices can remove at most the amount by which the density threshold drops, so $|B|>4k$.

If $r=5$ and $|B|\le4k$, the original strict density bound gives $|A|\ge4k+4$. There are only four possible vertices outside $B$, so $|A|\le4k+4$. Equality forces all of $6k+2,6k+3,6k+4,6k+5$ to belong to $A$. The second open claim asks for an ordering in this case, with the same odd length bound.

## Combine the two open claims {steps: close}

If the initial part has more than $4k$ entries, use the first claim there and retain the ordering in $A$. Otherwise the counting argument puts $A$ under the hypotheses of the second claim. Lean verifies this implication for every $n\ge18$. Applications of the two claims supply the actual orderings, so both claims remain obligations on this route.
