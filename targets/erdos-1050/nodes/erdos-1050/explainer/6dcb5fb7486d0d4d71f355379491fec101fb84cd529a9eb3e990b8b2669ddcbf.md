## Overview

The proof is the classical irrationality criterion, applied to a number $T$ for which another node supplies the approximations. Write $T = \sum_{m \ge 0} 1/(2^{m+3}-3)$. Node `erdos-1050--h1-v2` states that there are integer sequences $a_n, b_n$ with $b_n T - a_n \neq 0$ for every $n$ and $b_n T - a_n \to 0$. The proof takes those sequences, shows that the root's sum equals $T$, and then argues: if $T = p/q$ were rational, $q(b_n T - a_n) = b_n p - a_n q$ would be a nonzero integer for every $n$ that also tends to $0$, which is impossible. All the depth sits in `erdos-1050--h1-v2`; this file is short.

## The approximating sequences {steps: s1}

The proof opens by unpacking the statement of `erdos-1050--h1-v2` (in Lean, the theorem `erdos_1050__h1`, which the node's `Context.lean` provides): integer sequences $a, b$, the fact `hne` that $b_n T - a_n \ne 0$ for all $n$, and the fact `hlim` that $b_n T - a_n \to 0$.

## The root's series is T {steps: hT, hroot}

Step `hT` shows that $\sum_m 1/(2^{m+3}-3)$ is summable, by comparison with the geometric series $\tfrac14 (\tfrac12)^m$: each term is positive, because $2^{m+3} - 3 > 0$ (sub-steps `hT.s1.h1`, `hT.s1.h2`), and at most $1/(4 \cdot 2^m)$, because $4 \cdot 2^m \le 2^{m+3} - 3$ whenever $2^m \ge 1$ (sub-steps `hT.s2.h1`, `hT.s2.e`).

Step `hroot` shows that the root's sum $\sum_{n \ge 0} 1/(2^{n+1}-3)$ equals $T$. Shifting the index by two turns the root's series into the series of `hT`, so the root's series is summable too (`hroot.hs`). Its first two terms are $1/(2-3) = -1$ and $1/(4-3) = 1$, which cancel, and its tail from $n = 2$ on is exactly $T$. The proof then rewrites the goal from the irrationality of the root's sum to the irrationality of $T$.

## Suppose T is rational {steps: hq, hint}

Mathlib's `Irrational x` means $x$ is not the image of any rational number, so the proof assumes a rational $r$ whose value in $\mathbb{R}$ is $T$ (the `rintro` on the line before `hq`, which the outline does not list as a step) and derives a contradiction. Write $r = p/q$ in lowest terms, with numerator $p$ = `r.num` and denominator $q$ = `r.den`. Step `hq` records $q > 0$. Step `hint` shows, for every $n$, that the integer $b_n p - a_n q$, read as a real number, equals $q\,(b_n T - a_n)$; it uses $r = p/q$ (sub-step `hint.this`) and clears the denominator.

## The integers become small {steps: h0, s7, hsmall}

Step `h0` multiplies the limit `hlim` by the constant $q$: $q\,(b_n T - a_n) \to 0$. Step `s7` reads off the definition of a limit with $\varepsilon = 1$: there is an $N$ such that $|q\,(b_n T - a_n)| < 1$ for all $n \ge N$. Step `hsmall` takes $n = N$. By `hint`, this says the integer $b_N p - a_N q$ has absolute value less than $1$.

## The contradiction {steps: hnz, this}

Step `hnz` shows that the integer $b_N p - a_N q$ is not zero: if it were, `hint` at $N$ (sub-step `hnz.h2`) would give $q\,(b_N T - a_N) = 0$, and since $q \ne 0$, $b_N T - a_N = 0$, contradicting `hne`. Step `this` then uses that a nonzero integer has absolute value at least $1$. Together with `hsmall` (absolute value less than $1$), this is a contradiction, which `linarith` closes on the last line.
