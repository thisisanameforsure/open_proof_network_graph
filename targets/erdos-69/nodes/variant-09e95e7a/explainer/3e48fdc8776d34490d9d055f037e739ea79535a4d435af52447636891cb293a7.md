## Idea and method

The claim is that the real number $\sum_{n\ge 0} f(n)$ is irrational. The case splits recorded in the outline indicate that $f(n)$ is $(1/2)^n$ when $n$ is prime and $0$ otherwise. The proof is the classical argument by contradiction for sums of this kind.

Suppose the sum equals a rational $q$ with denominator $k$. Then go far enough out that a run of $k$ consecutive integers contains no primes. A factorial-shifted block works: each $j+N+1$ with $j<k$ has the proper divisor $j+2$.

Split the series into three parts:
- a finite head up to index $N$, which becomes an integer after multiplying by $2^N$;
- the block of $k$ vanishing terms;
- a tail $R$.

Multiplying by $2^N k$ turns the identity $q = \text{head} + R$ into an integer equal to $k\,2^N R$. That quantity is strictly positive, because a prime lies beyond the block. It is also strictly less than $1$, because the tail is dominated by a geometric series and $k<2^k$. No integer lies strictly between $0$ and $1$.

## Bounds on the terms and convergence {steps: f_nonneg f_le f_le.s1 f_le.s2 hs}

`f_nonneg` records $0 \le f(n)$ for all $n$.

`f_le` records $f(n) \le (1/2)^n$, by a case split on the defining condition of $f$:
- In the first case (`f_le.s1`) the goal is $(1/2)^n \le (1/2)^n$, closed by reflexivity.
- In the second case (`f_le.s2`) the goal is $0 \le (1/2)^n$, which is routine positivity.

From these two bounds, `hs` obtains that $f$ is summable. It uses the comparison test ("Comparison test of convergence of series of non-negative real numbers") against the geometric series $\sum (1/2)^n$, whose summability is `summable_geometric_of_lt_one`.

After these steps the goal is still that $\sum_n f(n)$ is irrational.

## A block of $k$ vanishing terms {steps: kpos comp comp.hd comp.hd.this comp.hd.e comp.s2 comp.s3 comp.s3.this}

From `kpos` on, the goal is `False`, so the proof proceeds by contradiction. The outline does not record the step that sets this up. From here on it works with:
- a rational $q$;
- a natural number $k$, which a later step (`hqk`) shows satisfies $q\cdot k = \operatorname{num}(q)$;
- natural numbers $N$ and $Z$;
- a real number $R$.

`kpos` records $0<k$.

`comp` claims that $f(j+N+1)=0$ for every $j<k$. (The statement is written with the index $j + k\cdot 0 + N+1$.) Its first child `hd` shows $j+2 \mid j+N+1$ from two facts:
- `comp.hd.this` gives $j+2 \mid (k+1)!$, via `Nat.dvd_factorial`, since $j+2\le k+1$.
- `comp.hd.e` gives the identity $j+N+1 = (k+1)! + (j+2)$.

Divisibility of the sum then follows from `dvd_add` and `dvd_rfl`.

Two cases then close the goal `False`, which concerns $j+N+1$ being prime:
- `comp.s2` is routine (linear arithmetic).
- `comp.s3` uses `comp.s3.this`, the positivity $0<(k+1)!$ from `Nat.factorial_pos`.

With primality excluded, `comp` evaluates $f$ by its "else" branch (`if_neg`) to $0$.

## Splitting the series {steps: hg hg' hg'.this split1 split2 zero_part zero_part.this split2'}

`hg` records that the shifted series $i\mapsto f(i+N+1)$ is summable, by `summable_nat_add_iff`. `hg'` records the same for $i \mapsto f(i+k+N+1)$, via its child `hg'.this` and reassociation of the index.

The series is then split twice, both times by `Summable.sum_add_tsum_nat_add`:
- `split1` gives $\sum_{i<N+1} f(i) + \sum_i f(i+N+1) = \sum_i f(i)$.
- `split2` gives $\sum_{i<k} f(i+N+1) + \sum_i f(i+k+N+1) = \sum_i f(i+N+1)$.

`zero_part` shows that the middle finite sum $\sum_{i<k} f(i+N+1)$ is $0$, by `Finset.sum_eq_zero`. Each term vanishes by `zero_part.this`, which applies `comp` to $j<k$ (membership in $\{0,\dots,k-1\}$ via `Finset.mem_range`).

`split2'` then concludes $\sum_i f(i+N+1) = R$, using `zero_add`. Here $R$ is the tail $\sum_i f(i+k+N+1)$.

## The tail is positive {steps: Rpos Rpos.s1 Rpos.hpi}

`Rpos` claims $0<R$.

Let $p$ be a prime with $k+N+1 \le p$, obtained in `Rpos.s1` from Euclid's theorem on the infinitude of primes (`Nat.exists_infinite_primes`). `Rpos.hpi` is the routine identity $p-(k+N+1)+k+N+1 = p$. This places $p$ as the index of a term of the tail.

That term is evaluated by the "then" branch (`if_pos`) and is positive. Since all terms of the tail are nonnegative, $R>0$.

## The tail is small {steps: geo geo.this Rle Rle.s1 Rle.s2 key key.h1 key.s2 key.s3}

`geo` records that $i\mapsto (1/2)^{i+k+N+1}$ is summable. Its child `geo.this` shows this for the reassociated exponent $i+(k+N+1)$, using `summable_nat_add_iff` and `summable_geometric_of_lt_one`.

`Rle` claims $R \le (1/2)^{k+N+1}\cdot 2$, by a two-step calculation:
- `Rle.s1` gives $R \le \sum_i (1/2)^{i+k+N+1}$ by termwise comparison (`Summable.tsum_le_tsum`).
- `Rle.s2` evaluates this sum as $(1/2)^{k+N+1}\cdot 2$. It uses `pow_add` to factor out $(1/2)^{k+N+1}$, then `tsum_mul_right`, then `tsum_geometric_two`, together with `add_assoc` and `mul_comm`.

`key` claims $2^N\bigl((1/2)^{k+N+1}\cdot 2\bigr) = (1/2)^k$:
- `key.h1` records $2^N(1/2)^N = 1$ (via `mul_pow`).
- `key.s2` rewrites the left side as $(1/2)^k\,\bigl(2^N (1/2)^N\bigr)\,\bigl(\tfrac12\cdot 2\bigr)$ (via `pow_add` and `pow_succ`).
- `key.s3` finishes the calculation. The outline records no claim for this step.

## The head is an integer after scaling {steps: hZ' hZ'.hi' hZ'.s2 hZ'.s2.e hZ'.s3}

`hZ'` claims $2^N \sum_{i<N+1} f(i) = Z$ as real numbers. It distributes $2^N$ over the sum (`Finset.mul_sum`) and compares termwise (`Finset.sum_congr`). For each $i$ in the range, with $i\le N$ recorded in `hZ'.hi'`, the goal becomes
$$2^N f(i) = \begin{cases} 2^{N-i} & \text{if } i \text{ is prime},\\ 0 & \text{otherwise.}\end{cases}$$

In the prime case (`hZ'.s2`) the goal is $2^N(1/2)^i = 2^{N-i}$. It follows from `hZ'.s2.e`, namely $2^N = 2^{N-i}\,2^i$ (via `pow_add` and `Nat.sub_add_cancel`), together with `mul_assoc` and `mul_pow`.

The remaining case `hZ'.s3`, $2^N\cdot 0 = 0$, is routine simplification.

## An integer strictly between $0$ and $1$ {steps: hqk hx hm mpos mpos.this mlt mlt.h2 mlt.hk2 mlt.hk0 mlt.s4 mlt.s5 mlt.s6 i1 i2}

`hqk` records $q\cdot k = \operatorname{num}(q)$ (`Rat.mul_den_eq_num`). `hx` records $q = \sum_{i<N+1} f(i) + R$.

From these, `hm` obtains for the integer $m = 2^N\operatorname{num}(q) - kZ$ the identity
$$m = k\,(2^N R) \quad\text{in } \mathbb{R}.$$

`mpos` shows $m>0$. With `mpos.this` giving $0<k$ as a real number and $R>0$ from before, the goal reduces to $0 < k\,(2^N R)$.

`mlt` shows $m<1$, so the goal becomes $k\,(2^N R) < 1$. Its children supply three inputs:
- `mlt.h2` gives $2^N R \le (1/2)^k$, using `mul_le_mul_of_nonneg_left`.
- `mlt.hk2` gives $k < 2^k$ (`Nat.lt_two_pow_self`).
- `mlt.hk0`, the bound $0\le k$, is routine positivity.

The chain then runs as follows:
- `mlt.s4` gives $k(2^N R) \le k(1/2)^k$.
- `mlt.s5` rewrites the right side as $k/2^k$ (`one_div_pow`, `mul_one_div`).
- `mlt.s6` gives $k/2^k<1$ (`div_lt_one`).

Finally, `i1` and `i2` transfer the two bounds to the integers: $0 < 2^N\operatorname{num}(q) - kZ$ and $2^N\operatorname{num}(q) - kZ < 1$. The remaining goal is `False`. No integer satisfies both bounds, so the assumption that the sum is rational is refuted.
