What an exception beyond van Doorn–Tang's range must look like, and why this statement is open.

This note proves nothing towards the statement. It records one elementary divisibility that every exception satisfies, and the two points at which the known methods stop. Each claim is marked **proved** (the argument is given here), **checked** (a finite computation, not a proof), **cited** (with the source and how it was read) or **heuristic**. No novelty is claimed. The set-up (write each $n-i$ as $a_ib_i$ with $a_i$ its part made of primes at most $k$; an exception needs $\prod a_i=k!$ and $b_j=1$ at the multiple of $k$) is that of Erdős, Lacampagne and Selfridge, Acta Arith. 49 (1988) 507–523, §2, read 2026-11-11. I did not find the divisibility of §1 stated there; I could not open their 1993 paper or Konyagin (1999) in full, and it may be in one of them.

**Notation.** An *exception* is a pair $(n,k)$ with $0<k$, $n\ge k^2$ and least prime factor of $\binom{n}{k}$ greater than $B=\lfloor n/k\rfloor$. The *window* is $W=\{n-k+1,\dots,n\}$, whose product is $k!\binom{n}{k}$. Write $m=kB$ for the multiple of $k$ in $W$ and $h=n-m=n \bmod k$. This statement asks that exceptions with $n\ge k^3$ and $n+1>\exp(\log^2k/(20\log\log k))$ have bounded $k$.

**1. A divisibility (proved).** *If $(n,k)$ is an exception then $\lfloor n/k\rfloor$ divides $\binom{k-1}{\,n \bmod k\,}$.*

Proof. Let $p$ be a prime dividing $m=kB$. Then $p\le B$ (a prime of $k$ is at most $k\le B$), so $p\nmid\binom{n}{k}$ and $v_p(\prod W)=v_p(k!)$. Now $v_p(\prod W)=\sum_{j\ge1}\#\{x\in W: p^j\mid x\}$ and $v_p(k!)=\sum_{j\ge1}\lfloor k/p^j\rfloor$, and $k$ consecutive integers hold at least $\lfloor k/p^j\rfloor$ multiples of $p^j$; so for every $j$ the window holds exactly $\lfloor k/p^j\rfloor$ of them. Write $W=\{m-A,\dots,m+h\}$ with $A=k-1-h$. If $p^j\mid m$, the multiples of $p^j$ in $W$ are the $m+i$ with $p^j\mid i$ and $-A\le i\le h$, and there are $\lfloor A/p^j\rfloor+\lfloor h/p^j\rfloor+1$ of them. Hence $\lfloor k/p^j\rfloor-\lfloor A/p^j\rfloor-\lfloor h/p^j\rfloor=1$ for $1\le j\le v_p(m)$. Each term $\lfloor k/p^j\rfloor-\lfloor A/p^j\rfloor-\lfloor h/p^j\rfloor$ is at least $0$ because $A+h=k-1$, and their sum over all $j$ is $v_p\bigl(k!/(A!\,h!)\bigr)$. So $v_p(m)\le v_p\bigl(k!/(A!\,h!)\bigr)$ for every prime $p$, that is $m$ divides $k!/(h!\,(k-1-h)!)=k\binom{k-1}{h}$. Divide by $k$. ∎

For the one known exception, $\binom{62}{6}$: $\lfloor 62/6\rfloor=10=\binom{5}{2}$, with equality. The divisibility has also been checked in Lean 4 with Mathlib on the network's fast checker, in the form `n / k ∣ (k - 1).choose (n % k)` from `0 < k`, `k * k ≤ n` and `(n.choose k).minFac > n / k`; that is not a gate verdict, and the Lean is not on the record.

Consequences (**proved** from the divisibility):

- $n<k\binom{k-1}{h}+k\le k\,2^{k-1}+k$. The hypothesis $k\lfloor n/k\rfloor\mid k!$ carried by this statement gives only $n<k!+k$.
- $m$ divides $\operatorname{lcm}(1,\dots,k)$, since $k\binom{k-1}{h}$ does (in the sum above each term is $0$ or $1$, and $0$ once $p^j>k$; this is the classical fact that $(N+1)\binom{N}{j}$ divides $\operatorname{lcm}(1,\dots,N+1)$, with $N=k-1$). So no prime power greater than $k$ divides $m$.
- If $k\mid n$ then $h=0$ and $B\mid 1$, impossible for $n\ge 2k$: this is the step proved on `erdos-1094--h2--h2--h3--h1`, by a different route.

**2. The same fact at every window element (proved).** Let $n\ge k$. For a real $y\ge 1$ and $0\le i<k$ let $s_i$ be the largest divisor of $n-i$ all of whose prime factors are at most $y$. Then
$$\text{no prime } p\le y \text{ divides } \tbinom{n}{k}\iff s_i \text{ divides } k\tbinom{k-1}{i}\ \text{ for every } 0\le i<k .$$
Proof. Fix a prime $p\le y$ and put $e_j=\#\{x\in W:p^j\mid x\}-\lfloor k/p^j\rfloor\in\{0,1\}$, so that $v_p\binom{n}{k}=\sum_j e_j$. For $x=n-i$ and $j\le v_p(x)$ the count of multiples of $p^j$ in $W$ is $\lfloor i/p^j\rfloor+\lfloor (k-1-i)/p^j\rfloor+1$, so the $j$-th term of $v_p\bigl(k\binom{k-1}{i}\bigr)=\sum_j\bigl(\lfloor k/p^j\rfloor-\lfloor i/p^j\rfloor-\lfloor (k-1-i)/p^j\rfloor\bigr)$ equals $1-e_j$. If all $e_j=0$ this gives $v_p(x)\le v_p\bigl(k\binom{k-1}{i}\bigr)$. Conversely let $j$ be the largest level with $e_j=1$ and take a multiple $x=n-i$ of $p^j$ in $W$ with $v=v_p(x)$ largest. No element of $W$ is divisible by $p^{t}$ for $t>v$, so $\lfloor k/p^t\rfloor=0$ there and those terms vanish; hence $v_p\bigl(k\binom{k-1}{i}\bigr)=v-\sum_{t\le v}e_t<v$. ∎

(**Checked** as well: both directions agree on all $564{,}442$ triples with $k\le 25$, $2k\le n<6000$, $y\in\{k/2,\,k,\,2k,\,\lfloor n/k\rfloor\}$.) With $y=B$ and $n\ge k^2$: an exception is a run of $k$ consecutive integers $n-i=s_i q_i$ in which $s_i$ divides $k\binom{k-1}{i}$ and $q_i$ is $1$ or a single prime greater than $B$ (two such primes would multiply past $n$), and $q_h=1$. This sharpens §3 of annex 9e0540ba on `erdos-1094--h2`, where the product of the $s_i$ is $k!$.

**3. Why the multiple of $k$ is the point (cited, heuristic).** Erdős, Lacampagne and Selfridge (1988, p. 522) observe: if $k$ divides $n-j$ then $b_j\le n/k$, so unless $b_j=1$ the coefficient has a prime factor at most $n/k$. That is the element $m$ here, and §1 says what $b_j=1$ costs. They also note (p. 523) that the bound $n/k$ should be attained: when every $a_i\le k$ and every $b_i$ is prime, the least prime factor is about $n/k$, and equal to $n/k$ when $k$ itself sits at the multiple of $k$; a generalisation of the twin prime conjecture predicts infinitely many such $n$ for each $k$. An explicit family (A. Meyerowitz, MathOverflow 368183, read 2026-11-11): if $p=q\,(k-1)!+1$ is prime and every $(pk-i)/(k-i)$, $0\le i<k$, is prime, then the least prime factor of $\binom{pk}{k}$ is exactly $p=n/k$. In that family every window element other than $m$ is (a number below $k$) × (a prime), exactly as in an exception. The only difference is at $m=n$: there $B=p$ is prime, while in an exception $B$ must divide $\binom{k-1}{h}$ (here $h=0$, so $B$ would have to be $1$). So, if such families exist, any proof of this statement has to use the element $m$: an argument that only counts window elements of the form (small) × (prime) cannot tell the two apart.

**4. Barrier A: below $g(k)$ this is Erdős problem 1095.** For $n\ge k^2$ an exception has no prime factor at most $k$, so $n\ge g(k)$, the least $n>k+1$ with that property. Known (**cited**): $k^{1+c}<g(k)\le\exp((1+o(1))k)$ (Ecklund, Erdős and Selfridge, Math. Comp. 28 (1974) 647–649, through erdosproblems.com/1095, read 2026-11-11); $g(k)\ge\exp(c\log^2k)$ (Konyagin, Mathematika 46 (1999) 41–55; abstract only, the paper is behind a paywall); the heuristic model of Sorenson, Sorenson and Webster gives $\log\hat g(k)\sim 0.788\,k/\log k$ (ANTS XIV, Open Book Series 4 (2020) 371–385, and the addendum arXiv:1907.08559v3, read 2026-11-11). So between the lower edge of this statement and $g(k)$ there is nothing to prove about primes above $k$: what is missing is a lower bound for $g(k)$, and Konyagin's has stood since 1999. Van Doorn and Tang's theorem (arXiv:2606.19863, read 2026-11-11), which cut this statement's range, follows Konyagin's method with primes in $(k,k+k^{21/40})$. The method stops near $\log n\asymp\log^2k$ for a reason one can read off the bound proved on `erdos-1094--h2--h2--h2--h3--h1`: with $r\approx\log n/\log k$ derivatives of $n/x$ its third term saves the factor $((r+1)\lambda/k)^{1/(2r)}\ge k^{-1/(2r)}$, and van Doorn and Tang need that below $1/\log k$, the density of primes; this forces $r<\log k/(2\log\log k)$. Once $r$ is of order $\log k$ the factor is bounded below and the bound says nothing. (Konyagin's own proof, which I have not read, reaches $\exp(c\log^2k)$ without the $\log\log k$. His reference list, on the journal's page, includes Baker and Harman's primes in short intervals (Proc. London Math. Soc. (3) 72 (1996) 261–280) and Bombieri and Pila, so it is of the same family; his fractional-parts theorem carries a parameter $W$ that van Doorn and Tang set to $1$.)

**5. Barrier B: above $g(k)$ the primes in $(k,n/k]$ must be forced into a specific window.** From $g(k)$ up to $k\,2^{k-1}$ windows with no prime factor at most $k$ in $\binom{n}{k}$ do exist ($n=g(k)$ is one, and the property depends only on $n$ modulo $\prod_{p\le k}p^{\lfloor\log_pk\rfloor+1}$), and an exception is such a window that also contains no multiple of any prime in $(k,B]$. By §1, for a given $k$ the candidates are the finitely many $n=kB+h$ with $B\mid\binom{k-1}{h}$: at most $k\,\tau(\operatorname{lcm}(1..k))=2^{(1+o(1))k/\log k}$ of them. **Heuristic:** each prime $q$ in $(k,B]$ misses a window of length $k$ with chance $1-k/q$, so a candidate survives with chance about $(\log k/\log B)^k\le 2^{-k}$ when $n\ge k^3$, and the expected number of exceptions for a given $k$ tends to $0$ faster than $2^{-k/2}$. A proof, though, has to treat each candidate, a single integer of size up to $2^k$: there is no average over $n$ to take, and none over $k$, since the statement is for all large $k$. Sieve bounds do not reach it either. An exception needs all but $O(k\log k/\log n)$ window elements to be (a number below $k$) × (a prime); Brun–Titchmarsh, applied to the primes in each interval $((n-k)/a,\,n/a]$, $a<k$, bounds the number of such elements only by about $2k\log\log k$, which is more than $k$. I know of no technique that shows a given window at height $\exp(k^{c})$ contains a multiple of a prime in $(k,n/k]$.

**6. What this leaves.** Given Konyagin's bound (which puts $g(k)$ above this statement's lower edge for large $k$), the statement says: for large $k$, every $\binom{n}{k}$ with $n\ge k^3$ and no prime factor at most $k$ has a prime factor in $(k,n/k]$. Its lower edge moves up with any new lower bound for $g(k)$; its upper edge is $k\,2^{k-1}+k$ by §1. Nothing here reduces the range in between.

Numerical note (**checked**, a finite search, not evidence about large $k$): a scan of all $k<60$, $k^2\le n<200000$ by Kummer's criterion finds the single exception $(62,6)$, in agreement with the annexes on `erdos-1094`.
