## The open core of Erdős 1094 above exp(c·log²k): what is provable and what is not

**Setting.** This node asks: for every c > 0 there is K such that whenever 0 < k, k³ ≤ n, exp(c·log²k) < n and the least prime factor of C(n,k) exceeds ⌊n/k⌋, we have k ≤ K. Everything above exp(c·log²k) is beyond Konyagin's range, so no library result applies; this is the open core of the problem on the side n ≥ k².

**Step 1 (provable, the sieve step).** Let p be a prime with k < p ≤ ⌊n/k⌋. If p divides some m with n − k < m ≤ n, then p divides the falling product n(n−1)···(n−k+1) = k!·C(n,k); since p > k, p does not divide k!, so p divides C(n,k) and the least prime factor of C(n,k) is at most p ≤ ⌊n/k⌋, contradicting the hypothesis. Hence under the hypotheses of the node **no integer in (n−k, n] has a prime factor in (k, ⌊n/k⌋]**.

**Step 2 (the hole `h_core`).** The node with that conclusion added as a hypothesis: the k consecutive integers n−k+1, …, n each factor as a·b with a k-smooth and every prime factor of b greater than n/k (so b = 1 or b > n/k, whence a < k). Showing this is impossible for large k is the open problem: a sieve heuristic gives about k·log k/log(n/k) integers of an interval of length k free of primes in (k, n/k], which is below 1 only once n/k exceeds roughly k^k, far above exp(c·log²k).

**A further provable piece, not carved here.** Under the same hypotheses the k-smooth parts a_m of the k consecutive integers satisfy ∏ a_m = k! exactly: the product of the m is k!·C(n,k), and C(n,k) has no prime factor ≤ k (its least prime factor exceeds n/k ≥ k²). So the smooth parts are, on average, as small as the factorials' — the same shape as Erdős's observation that k consecutive integers have "too few" small prime factors when none of them carries a prime between k and n/k. A next skeleton could state that identity as a second hole beside the core; it is provable in Lean by comparing p-adic valuations.

**What would close the core.** A theorem of the form: for every c > 0 and all large k, every interval of k consecutive integers above exp(c·log²k) contains a multiple of some prime in (k, n/k]. This is a statement about primes in short intervals combined with the sieve, and is not known; Konyagin's method reaches exp(c·log²k) only.
