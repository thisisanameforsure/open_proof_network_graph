# The leaf in residue form, and the shape of the window

Notation. $B = \lfloor n/k\rfloor$, $W = \{n-k+1,\dots,n\}$ (the $k$ factors of $n(n-1)\cdots(n-k+1) = k!\binom nk$), and $m = kB$, the multiple of $k$ in $W$. The leaf's hypotheses are $0<k$, $k^3\le n$, $\exp(\log^2 k/(20\log\log k)) < n+1$, $m \mid k!$, $\operatorname{minFac}\binom nk > B$, $n/\gcd(n,k)$ prime and $k\nmid n$; the goal is a bound on $k$.

## Two residue facts

**(R1)** For a prime $p\le k$: $p\nmid\binom nk$ iff adding $k$ and $n-k$ in base $p$ carries nowhere (Kummer), i.e. the window $W$ contains exactly $\lfloor k/p^i\rfloor$ multiples of $p^i$ for every $i$.

**(R2)** For a prime $p>k$: $p\mid\binom nk$ iff $n \bmod p < k$ iff $p$ has a multiple in $W$. Indeed $p\nmid k!$, so $p\mid\binom nk$ iff $p\mid n(n-1)\cdots(n-k+1)$ iff $p\mid n-j$ for some $j<k$, and the one $j<p$ with $p\mid n-j$ is $j = n\bmod p$.

So $\operatorname{minFac}\binom nk > B$ is equivalent to: (R1) at every prime $p\le k$, together with $n\bmod p\ge k$ at every prime $k<p\le B$. The partial on this node proves this direction in Lean and leaves the leaf in that residue form as its one hole; nothing is lost, and nothing is gained beyond the form in which every analytic attack (van Doorn–Tang, Konyagin) reads the hypothesis.

## What the window looks like under the hypotheses

Write each $N\in W$ as $N = s_N\,r_N$ with $s_N$ its $k$-smooth part. Since no prime in $(k,B]$ divides any element of $W$ (R2), every prime factor of $r_N$ exceeds $B$.

- $B^2\ge n$: from $n\ge k^3$ we get $B\ge k^2$, so $B^2\ge k^2B = k\cdot kB\ge k(n-k+1)\ge n$ for $k\ge 2$. Hence an integer $\le n$ all of whose prime factors exceed $B$ is $1$ or a single prime.
- So every $N\in W$ is either **$k$-smooth**, or **$N = s_N\,P_N$ with $P_N > B$ prime and $s_N < k$** (because $s_N P_N = N \le n < k(B+1)\le kP_N$).
- The $k$-smooth part of $\prod_{N\in W} N = k!\binom nk$ is exactly $k!$, because no prime $\le k\le B$ divides $\binom nk$: $\prod_{N\in W} s_N = k!$.
- $m = kB$ is itself $k$-smooth ($m\mid k!$, which is where $n < k!+k$ comes from, as in the proof of `erdos-1094--h1`), and every $k$-smooth element is $\ge n-k+1$, so there are at most $\log(k!)/\log(n-k+1)$ of them.
- $n = gP$ with $P = n/\gcd(n,k)$ prime and $g = \gcd(n,k)$ a proper divisor of $k$ ($k\nmid n$); so $n\in W$ has $s_n = g$ and $P > B$.

## Why the residue constraints at primes $\le k$ cannot finish alone

The postmortem on this node (t1111-a, 2026-11-11) records that every constraint it used concerns primes up to $k$, and all of them hold with equality at the known exception $(n,k)=(62,6)$: $57=3\cdot19$, $58=2\cdot29$, $59$, $60$, $61$, $62=2\cdot31$, with smooth parts $3,2,1,60,1,2$ multiplying to $6! $. Any proof of the hole must use the size hypotheses: $n\ge k^3$ and the exponential lower bound on $n$ (where van Doorn–Tang's use of Konyagin's theorem stops), or the upper bound $n< k!+k$ that $m\mid k!$ gives, and it has to produce a prime $p\in(k,B]$ with a multiple in $W$, i.e. with $n\bmod p<k$.

## A heuristic on the remaining range (not a proof)

For a random $N$ near $n$, the chance that $N$ is $k$-smooth or $s\cdot P$ with $s<k$ and $P>B$ prime is about $\sum_{s<k}\frac{1}{s\log(n/s)}\approx \frac{\log k}{\log n}$. Treating the $k$ elements of $W$ as independent, the expected number of exceptional $n$ in the leaf's range is about $\sum_{n\le k!}(\log k/\log n)^k$, which is dominated by $n\approx k!$ and is of size $k!\,(\log k/(k\log k))^k\approx k!/k^k\approx e^{-k}$; at the lower end $n\approx\exp(c\log^2 k)$ the summand is $(c\log k)^{-k}$. So, heuristically, the whole range from the exponential bound up to $k!$ should hold no exception for large $k$, with the thinnest margin at the top end $n\approx k!$, where the window is forced to be a product of $k$ numbers $s_N<k$ (no smooth element can survive $n > k!$, and $n<k!+k$ leaves only $m$ itself). A proof along these lines would need a lower bound on the number of primes $p\in(k,B]$ with $n\bmod p<k$ that is uniform in $n$ up to $k!$; Konyagin-type counting gives it only up to $\exp(\log^2k/(20\log\log k))$.
